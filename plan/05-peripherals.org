:PROPERTIES:
:WEEK: 20
:CUSTOM_ID: week-20

** Learn
A datasheet is a part's API documentation, and like API docs it buries the
three facts you need among forty pages you don't. Learn to find them fast:
supply voltage range and current, pinout, communication interface and
address, timing requirements, and absolute maximum ratings (the limits that
destroy the part, not ones you design to). Breakout boards add their own
layer: a regulator, level shifters, or pull-up resistors that change what you
need to do.

- Bosch: [[https://www.bosch-sensortec.com/products/environmental-sensors/humidity-sensors-bme280/][BME280 product page]] (download the datasheet from here)
- Becky Stern: [[https://beckystern.com/2025/10/28/how-to-read-a-datasheet/][How to read a datasheet]] (article and video)

- [ ] Find the absolute maximum ratings and explain how they differ from operating conditions
- [ ] Look at your breakout board's schematic (or the seller's page): does it have a regulator, level shifters, or I²C pull-ups?
- [ ] Do the same for an SSD1306 128×64 OLED module
- [ ] Draw the wiring for both on the Pico's I²C0 pins before connecting anything
** Parts

For a new sensor, which three pieces of information must you find before you
connect it to a Pico?

* TODO Week 21: I²C and the logic analyzer
:PROPERTIES:
:WEEK: 21
:CUSTOM_ID: week-21
:END:
** Learn
I²C uses two shared wires: SDA (data) and SCL (clock). Many devices sit on the
same pair, each with a 7-bit address. The lines are open-drain: devices only
pull them low, and pull-up resistors bring them high. Missing pull-ups are a
classic reason I²C "doesn't work". A logic analyzer records digital lines
over time, and PulseView's I²C decoder turns the waveform into "write to
0x76, register 0xD0" so you can see the protocol rather than guess at it.

- SparkFun: [[https://learn.sparkfun.com/tutorials/i2c][I²C]]
- [[https://docs.micropython.org/en/latest/library/machine.I2C.html][MicroPython =machine.I2C=]]
- [[https://sigrok.org/wiki/PulseView][PulseView]] and its [[https://sigrok.org/wiki/Protocol_decoder:I2c][I²C decoder]]
** Build [0/6]
- [ ] Wire the BME280 to I²C0 and run =i2c.scan()=; confirm the address matches the datasheet
- [ ] Read the chip ID register (0xD0) by hand with =readfrom_mem= and check it's 0x60
- [ ] Capture that transaction with the logic analyzer and decode it in PulseView; label start, address, ACK, data, and stop in your notes
- [ ] Read temperature and humidity using a MicroPython BME280 driver
- [ ] Add the OLED on the same bus and show the readings on it
- [ ] Measure the I²C clock frequency in PulseView and compare with what you configured
** Parts
- BME280 breakout and SSD1306 OLED from week 20
- USB logic analyzer (8-channel, sigrok-compatible) and PulseView
** Checkpoint
=i2c.scan()= returns an empty list. List the likely causes in the order you'd
check them.
#+begin_details
#+html: <summary>Check your answer</summary>
1. Power and ground: is the sensor getting 3.3 V, and does it share the
   Pico's ground?
2. SDA and SCL swapped, or wired to pins that aren't the I²C instance you
   configured.
3. No pull-ups on SDA and SCL. Check the breakout; add 4.7 kΩ to 3.3 V if
   there are none.
4. The device at a different address, or held in reset by an address or
   CS pin.
5. A bad jumper wire or breadboard contact. Check with continuity.
The logic analyzer shows you directly whether the clock toggles and whether
anyone ACKs.
#+end_details

* TODO Week 22: SPI and an SD card
:PROPERTIES:
:WEEK: 22
:CUSTOM_ID: week-22
:END:
** Learn
SPI uses separate lines for clock (SCK), data out (MOSI or TX), and data in
(MISO or RX), plus one chip-select (CS) line per device. There are no
addresses: you pick a device by pulling its CS low. It's faster and simpler
than I²C but costs more pins. An SD card speaks SPI, which makes it a natural
log store for the capstone.

- SparkFun: [[https://learn.sparkfun.com/tutorials/serial-peripheral-interface-spi][Serial Peripheral Interface]]
- [[https://docs.micropython.org/en/latest/library/machine.SPI.html][MicroPython =machine.SPI=]]
- micropython-lib: [[https://github.com/micropython/micropython-lib/tree/master/micropython/drivers/storage/sdcard][SD card driver]]
** Build [0/5]
- [ ] Wire a 3.3 V-compatible microSD breakout to SPI0 and mount the card with the =sdcard= driver
- [ ] Write a text file, unplug, and read it on your laptop
- [ ] Capture the SPI traffic during a write with the logic analyzer and identify CS, clock, and data
- [ ] Measure how long it takes to append one line, and how long to write 100 lines in one go
- [ ] Write a table in your notes comparing I²C, SPI, and UART: wires, addressing, speed, typical uses
** Parts
- microSD card breakout that works at 3.3 V (many cheap 5 V-only modules don't; check the regulator and level shifter)
- A small microSD card (any size; format FAT32)
** Checkpoint
You have three SPI devices and one I²C bus. How many signal wires does each
need, and what does that suggest about when to choose each?
#+begin_details
#+html: <summary>Check your answer</summary>
SPI with three devices needs SCK, MOSI, and MISO (shared) plus three CS lines:
six wires. I²C needs two wires for any number of devices with different
addresses. SPI is faster and simpler (good for displays, SD cards, fast ADCs).
I²C saves pins and suits slower sensors.
#+end_details

* TODO Week 23: UART between machines
:PROPERTIES:
:WEEK: 23
:CUSTOM_ID: week-23
:END:
** Learn
UART is the oldest of the three: two wires (TX to the other side's RX, and
the reverse), no clock, and both sides agreeing on a baud rate in advance.
It's how many GPS modules and debug consoles talk, and how two boards can
exchange data with almost no setup. The Debug Probe's UART gives the laptop a
serial port. The Raspberry Pi 3B (mrshudson) has a 3.3 V UART on its GPIO
header, so the two boards can be wired directly with a shared ground.

- SparkFun: [[https://learn.sparkfun.com/tutorials/serial-communication][Serial communication]]
- [[https://docs.micropython.org/en/latest/library/machine.UART.html][MicroPython =machine.UART=]]
- Raspberry Pi: [[https://www.raspberrypi.com/documentation/computers/configuration.html][Configuration]] (the UART section: enabling the serial port and turning off the login console)
** Build [0/5]
- [ ] Send sensor readings from the Pico's UART0 to the laptop through the Debug Probe and read them with =minicom=
- [ ] Capture a few bytes on the logic analyzer and decode them; find the start and stop bits
- [ ] Set the wrong baud rate on one side and describe what you receive
- [ ] Optional: wire the Pico to mrshudson's GPIO14/15 (TX/RX crossed, grounds joined) and read lines with Python on the Pi
- [ ] Define a simple line format (e.g. =T=21.4,H=56.2=) and parse it on the receiving side
** Parts
- Debug Probe (UART port), logic analyzer
- Female-to-female jumpers if you connect to mrshudson
** Checkpoint
Why do you connect TX to RX (crossed), and why must the grounds be connected
even though no "ground signal" is sent?
#+begin_details
#+html: <summary>Check your answer</summary>
Each side transmits on TX and listens on RX, so one side's TX has to reach
the other's RX. Voltages only mean something relative to a reference. Without
a shared ground, the receiver can't tell what the sender considers high and
low, and the signal is garbage, or worse, current finds another path.
#+end_details

:PROPERTIES:
:WEEK: 24

** Learn
Logging data well is harder than reading it once. How often should you
sample? Faster than the thing changes, and no faster than you can store and
trust. What happens when the sensor is unplugged, or returns an impossible
value? A device left alone overnight has to handle that without crashing. This
week combines the sensor, display, and SD card into the first version of the
StarFest conditions monitor.

- Your week 20–22 notes
- The capstone page, for the v1 requirements
- Bosch BME280 datasheet: the section on self-heating and recommended modes for weather monitoring
** Build [0/6]
- [ ] Capstone v1: read temperature, humidity, and pressure at a chosen interval
- [ ] Capstone v1: show the current readings on the OLED
- [ ] Capstone v1: append timestamped readings to a CSV on the SD card
- [ ] Capstone v1: unplug the sensor while running; the device shows an error and recovers when it's reconnected
- [ ] Reject impossible values (e.g. humidity above 100%) and count them
- [ ] Run it overnight; check the log for gaps and explain any you find
** Parts
- Everything from weeks 20–22
** Checkpoint
Your overnight log has a 40-minute gap at 3 a.m. List three plausible causes
and how you would tell them apart.
#+begin_details
#+html: <summary>Check your answer</summary>
1. The program crashed or hung, then a watchdog or reboot restarted it. A
   log line at boot or a reboot counter would show this.
2. The sensor stopped responding. Your error counter or error log entries

3. SD card writes failed or stalled. Check the write-error count, and whether

Power dips would show as reboots too. Logging a boot message and keeping

:PROPERTIES:

** Learn
The Pico 2 W's wireless chip connects over SPI internally, and MicroPython
wraps it in the =network= module. Sending readings to a server means
thinking about what happens when Wi-Fi drops: retry, buffer, or accept the
loss. On mrshudson you'll write the receiving side yourself: a small Python
service that accepts readings, stores them, and serves a page with recent
values and a dew-point warning. This is software you know how to write; the
new part is the device on the other end being unreliable.

- [[https://datasheets.raspberrypi.com/picow/connecting-to-the-internet-with-pico-w.pdf][Connecting to the internet with Raspberry Pi Pico W-series]] (PDF)
- [[https://docs.micropython.org/en/latest/library/network.WLAN.html][MicroPython =network.WLAN=]]
- Wikipedia: [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dew_point][Dew point]] (the Magnus formula section)

** Checkpoint
Why does a dew-point warning matter for a night of observing, and why do
temperature and humidity alone not tell you whether the sky will be clear?
#+begin_details
#+html: <summary>Check your answer</summary>
When a surface cools to the dew point, water condenses on it: telescope
optics and eyepieces fog up. A warning tells you to start dew heaters or use
dew shields. Clear skies depend on cloud cover, transparency (haze, smoke,
humidity higher up), and seeing (turbulence aloft). A ground-level
temperature and humidity sensor measures none of these directly.
#+end_details
