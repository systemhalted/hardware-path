* TODO Week 26: Regulators, decoupling, and current budgets
:PROPERTIES:
:WEEK: 26
:CUSTOM_ID: week-26
:END:
** Learn
Every circuit needs a stable supply at the right voltage. A linear regulator
(LDO) burns the difference between input and output as heat: simple and
quiet, but wasteful when the gap is large. A switching regulator (buck,
boost, or buck-boost) converts efficiently but adds ripple and design care.
The Pico has a buck-boost converter that accepts 1.8–5.5 V on VSYS, which is
why it runs from USB or batteries. Decoupling capacitors (typically 100 nF
right next to each chip's supply pin) supply the fast current spikes the
regulator can't. A current budget adds up what everything draws, and it tells
you how long a battery lasts.

- [[https://datasheets.raspberrypi.com/picow/pico-2-w-datasheet.pdf][Pico 2 W datasheet]]: the power section (VSYS, VBUS, the Schottky diode, powering from batteries)
- SparkFun: [[https://learn.sparkfun.com/tutorials/capacitors][Capacitors]] (the decoupling application section)
- SparkFun: [[https://learn.sparkfun.com/tutorials/battery-technologies][Battery technologies]]
- SparkFun: [[https://learn.sparkfun.com/tutorials/how-to-power-a-project][How to power a project]]
- Watch: MIT 6.002 [[https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-002-circuits-and-electronics-spring-2007/resources/lecture-22/][lecture 22, Energy and power]]
- Watch: MIT 6.002 [[https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-002-circuits-and-electronics-spring-2007/resources/lecture-23/][lecture 23, Energy, CMOS]] (why a lower clock saves power)
** Build [0/5]

- [ ] Write a current budget table and estimate how long 3×AA cells (about 2000 mAh) would run it

- [ ] Read the datasheet section on using VSYS and USB together, and explain the role of the diode

** Checkpoint
A linear regulator drops 5 V to 3.3 V at 200 mA. How much power does it turn
into heat, and what's its efficiency?
#+begin_details
#+html: <summary>Check your answer</summary>
P = (5 − 3.3) × 0.2 = 0.34 W of heat. Efficiency is 3.3 / 5 = 66%. A buck
converter would typically manage 85–95%, which matters most on batteries.
#+end_details

** Learn
A good joint is shiny and concave, like a little volcano, and it wets both the
pad and the lead. You get it by heating the pad and the lead together, then
feeding solder into the joint, not onto the iron. Flux does the real work of
cleaning the metal; leaded solder (63/37) is easier to learn with, lead-free
is safer to handle. A practice kit gives you dozens of joints to get wrong
cheaply.

- Adafruit: [[https://learn.adafruit.com/adafruit-guide-excellent-soldering][Adafruit guide to excellent soldering]] (the common problems page is the most useful)
- SparkFun: [[https://learn.sparkfun.com/tutorials/how-to-solder-through-hole-soldering][How to solder: through-hole soldering]]
- [[https://pine64.org/devices/pinecil/][Pinecil]] soldering iron
** Build [0/5]
- [ ] Set up a ventilated space, tip cleaner, flux, and safety glasses
- [ ] Solder a through-hole practice kit (any small kit with 20 or more joints)
- [ ] Inspect every joint against the Adafruit common-problems photos and redo the bad ones
- [ ] Desolder and replace one component with solder wick
- [ ] Solder headers onto a breakout board you own that came without them (or a spare)
** Parts
- Soldering iron (Pinecil or similar with temperature control), stand, brass tip cleaner
- Solder (0.8 mm) and a flux pen
- Solder wick
- A small through-hole practice kit
- Safety glasses
** Checkpoint
Your joint looks dull, grainy, and balled up around the lead without flowing
onto the pad. What went wrong and how do you fix it?
#+begin_details
#+html: <summary>Check your answer</summary>
It's a cold joint: the pad and lead weren't hot enough for the solder to wet
them, or the part moved while it cooled. Add a little flux, reheat the pad
and lead together, let the solder flow, and hold everything still until it
solidifies.
#+end_details

** Learn
Moving a circuit from a breadboard to a soldered protoboard forces you to plan
the layout: where parts go, which way power runs, how to keep wires short.
That planning is the same thinking as PCB layout next phase, done by hand.
Socket the Pico in female headers so you can reuse it.

- Your capstone v1/v2 schematic (update it first if it drifted on the breadboard)
- Adafruit soldering guide, for reference
** Build [0/5]
- [ ] Draw the final capstone schematic, including power, decoupling capacitors, and pull-ups if needed
- [ ] Plan the protoboard layout on paper (or squared paper) before cutting or soldering anything
- [ ] Solder female headers for the Pico and the modules, then the wiring
- [ ] Check every power and ground connection with continuity before first power-on
- [ ] Capstone: run the full v2 firmware on the soldered board for 24 hours
** Parts
- Protoboard (perfboard or stripboard), around 7×9 cm
- Female header strips
- Solid-core hookup wire
** Checkpoint
Why check continuity between the 3.3 V and ground rails *before* first
power-on, and what reading do you hope to see?
#+begin_details
#+html: <summary>Check your answer</summary>
A solder bridge or misplaced wire between power and ground is a short. At
power-on it can overheat parts, trip the USB port's protection, or damage
the regulator. You want no continuity beep. (A brief beep that fades is fine:
the decoupling capacitors are charging from the meter.)
#+end_details
