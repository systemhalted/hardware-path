Weeks 29–32. By the end you have designed a printed circuit board for the
capstone, ordered it, assembled it, and brought it up. Taking a design from
schematic to a working board is the core skill of hardware engineering. The
board is deliberately simple: the Pico and the modules sit in sockets, and
your PCB replaces the protoboard wiring.

Order the board at the end of week 31. Fabrication and shipping usually take
one to three weeks, so start Phase 8 while you wait and come back for week 32.

** Learn
KiCad's schematic editor is where the design becomes a precise, checkable
description: every part has a symbol, every connection is a net, and the
electrical rules check (ERC) catches unconnected pins and power nets with no
source. Each symbol gets a footprint: the copper pattern it will solder to.
Good schematics read like good code, with named nets, power symbols instead
of long wires, and sections laid out by function.

- [[https://docs.kicad.org/][KiCad documentation]]: "Getting Started in KiCad", the schematic chapters
- [[https://www.youtube.com/@PhilsLab][Phil's Lab]] on YouTube: his KiCad schematic videos
- Your week 28 schematic

- [ ] Draw the capstone schematic: Pico socket, BME280 and OLED headers, microSD module header, decoupling, power input

- [ ] Assign a footprint to every symbol; use two 1×20 female header footprints for the Pico, spaced to match its mechanical drawing

** Checkpoint
What's the difference between a symbol and a footprint, and why does a wrong
footprint cost more than a wrong symbol?
#+begin_details
#+html: <summary>Check your answer</summary>
A symbol is the part's logical representation in the schematic: pins and
their names. A footprint is the physical copper layout on the board: pad
sizes, spacing, and pin numbering. A symbol mistake is usually caught by ERC
or review. A wrong footprint (wrong spacing, or a mirrored pin order) gets
manufactured, and you learn about it when the part doesn't fit or doesn't
work.
#+end_details

:WEEK: 30
:CUSTOM_ID: week-30
:END:
** Learn
Layout turns the netlist into copper. Place parts first (that's most of the
work), then route. Keep decoupling capacitors next to the pins they serve, use
a ground plane (a whole layer, or a pour, of ground), keep high-current and
signal paths sensible, and leave room for mounting holes and labels. The
Pico 2 W's Wi-Fi antenna sits at the end opposite the USB connector: the
datasheet asks for an area of about 14 × 9 mm around it with no copper on
any layer, and recommends placing it at the edge of your board. Trace
width depends on current; for this board, signal traces of 0.25 mm and power
traces of 0.5 mm or more are comfortable.

- [[https://docs.kicad.org/][KiCad documentation]]: "Getting Started in KiCad", the PCB editor chapters
- [[https://www.youtube.com/@PhilsLab][Phil's Lab]]: his PCB layout and grounding videos
** Build [0/6]
- [ ] Set the board outline (around 70 × 50 mm) and add four mounting holes
- [ ] Place the Pico with its antenna end at the board edge, and add a keep-out zone (no copper on any layer) under and around the antenna as the Pico 2 W datasheet specifies
- [ ] Place parts so that the OLED is visible and the sensor sits away from the Pico (it warms up)
- [ ] Route all nets on two layers, with a ground pour on the bottom that stops at the antenna keep-out
- [ ] Add silkscreen labels: pin names on headers, the board name, your initials, a version number
- [ ] Print the layout at 1:1 on paper and check the real Pico and modules fit the footprints
** Parts
- Paper and a printer (for the 1:1 fit check)
** Checkpoint
Why place the BME280 away from the Pico and the regulator, and why does the
ground pour help?
#+begin_details
#+html: <summary>Check your answer</summary>
The Pico, especially with Wi-Fi active, and its regulator warm the board
nearby. A sensor beside them reads high and its humidity reading drifts, so
distance (or a slot in the board) gives truer readings. A ground pour gives
every part a short, low-impedance return path. It reduces noise and makes
routing easier because ground connections only need a via.
#+end_details

:WEEK: 31
:CUSTOM_ID: week-31
:END:
** Learn
The design rule check (DRC) enforces the fab's limits: minimum trace width,
spacing, drill sizes, and annular rings. Set KiCad's rules to match your
manufacturer before you run it. Gerbers (one file per layer) plus a drill file
are what the fab actually builds from. Their online viewer is your last
chance to catch a missing layer or a flipped silkscreen.

- [[https://jlcpcb.com/capabilities/pcb-capabilities][JLCPCB PCB capabilities]] (set your design rules from these; PCBWay publishes similar ones)
- [[https://docs.kicad.org/][KiCad documentation]]: "Getting Started in KiCad", the fabrication outputs section
** Build [0/5]
- [ ] Enter the fab's minimum trace, spacing, and drill values into KiCad's board setup
- [ ] Run DRC and fix everything; write down any rule you had to look up
- [ ] Export Gerbers and the drill file, and inspect them in KiCad's Gerber viewer
- [ ] Upload to the fab, compare their preview with your design, and order five boards
- [ ] Record the order date; start Phase 8 while waiting
** Parts
- PCB order (about $5–15 including slow shipping)
** Checkpoint
The fab's minimum trace spacing is 0.127 mm (5 mil). Why might you still set
your own rules wider, say 0.2 mm?
#+begin_details
#+html: <summary>Check your answer</summary>
Designing at the fab's absolute limit leaves no margin for manufacturing
variation, which raises the chance of shorts or defects. It also makes hand
soldering and rework harder. A simple board like this has plenty of room, so
wider rules cost nothing and make the board more forgiving.
#+end_details

** Learn
Bring-up is powering a new board for the first time, carefully and in stages.
First inspect it and check for shorts with no power. Then power it with
nothing plugged in and measure the rails. Then add one part at a time,
confirming each works before adding the next. When something's wrong, the
staged approach tells you where.

- Your week 28 continuity checklist
- Your week 21 I²C debugging notes
** Build [0/6]
- [ ] Inspect the bare boards against your design; check 3V3-to-GND and VSYS-to-GND for shorts
- [ ] Solder the female headers and any passive parts
- [ ] Power the board with only the Pico fitted; measure 3.3 V at every module header
- [ ] Add modules one at a time: OLED, then BME280, then microSD, testing each with a small script
- [ ] Capstone v3: run the full firmware on your PCB for 24 hours
- [ ] List what you'd change in a version 2 of the board, and add a short "errata" section to your notes
** Parts

- Female headers (from week 28 stock)
** Checkpoint
You plug in the new board and the Pico's LED doesn't come on, though it did
on the protoboard. What do you check first, without removing anything?
#+begin_details
#+html: <summary>Check your answer</summary>
Unplug it, then check for a short between 3V3 and GND, and between VSYS and
GND, with continuity mode. A short is the most common fault, and it can also
pull the supply down. If there's no short, power it and measure VBUS, VSYS,
and 3V3 at the Pico's pins. Then check that the Pico sits in its sockets the
right way round, and that the footprint spacing and pin-1 position match.
#+end_details
