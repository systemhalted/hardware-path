#+TITLE: Start here

This is a 36-week path into hardware engineering for a software engineer. It
moves from Ohm's law to a printed circuit board you design yourself, and ends
by building a CPU out of NAND gates in a simulator. Every week pairs a concept
with a build, and every build starts with a prediction you check with a meter.

The plan assumes about 3–4 hours a week. Weeks are milestones, not deadlines.
If a week takes two, it takes two.

* How to use this repo

The Org files in =plan/= are the record. The site is generated from them on
every push, so the files always win.

- Each week is a top-level heading with a TODO state. Change it to =DOING=
  when you start (=C-c C-t=) and =DONE= when you finish. Optional weeks can be
  marked =SKIPPED=.
- Tick build items as you go (=C-c C-c= on a checkbox). The =[/]= counter on
  each =Build= heading updates itself.
- Write your notes under the week's =Notes= heading. Short is fine.
- Commit and push. The dashboard updates in a minute or two.
- From your phone, the GitHub mobile app can edit a file and commit it, which
  is enough for ticking a box or jotting a note at the bench.

To preview the site locally: =make site=, then open =_site/index.html=.
=make check= confirms the files still have the structure the site expects.

* A week's rhythm

| Time          | Activity                                                       |
|---------------+----------------------------------------------------------------|
| 30–45 minutes | Read or watch the Learn material                               |
| 60–90 minutes | Build and test the circuit                                     |
| 30 minutes    | Change one thing, predict what will happen, then test          |
| 15–30 minutes | Record schematic, measurements, code, and what surprised you   |

* The four notes

Every week has the same four note headings. They turn a tutorial into
something you learned.

1. *Expected*: what you predicted before powering it up.
2. *Measured*: what the meter, the scope, or the program actually showed.
3. *Different and why*: where those disagreed and your best explanation.
4. *Next*: what you would change or try next.

A habit that pays off: after finishing a build, put the guide away and rebuild
it from your own schematic and notes.

* The board

The plan uses the *Raspberry Pi Pico 2 WH*: the Pico 2 W with headers already
soldered. It has the RP2350 chip (two Arm Cortex-M33 cores and two RISC-V
cores), Wi-Fi and Bluetooth, and Raspberry Pi's documentation, which is among
the best for any microcontroller. You start in MicroPython and move to C with
the official SDK.

- [[https://www.raspberrypi.com/documentation/microcontrollers/pico-series.html][Pico-series documentation]]
- [[https://datasheets.raspberrypi.com/picow/pico-2-w-datasheet.pdf][Pico 2 W datasheet]] (pinout, power, electrical limits)
- [[https://datasheets.raspberrypi.com/rp2350/rp2350-datasheet.pdf][RP2350 datasheet]] (the chip itself; you will dip into it from Phase 4)

* Tools and when to buy them

There is no kit to buy up front. Each week lists the parts it needs. These are
the tools, in the order you need them:

| When     | Tool                                         | Why                                                        |
|----------+----------------------------------------------+------------------------------------------------------------|
| Week 1   | Digital multimeter (auto-ranging, with continuity beep) | Every build starts with a measurement            |
| Week 1   | Two full-size breadboards and jumper wires   | Solderless prototyping                                     |
| Week 1   | Pico 2 WH and a USB cable that carries data  | Power source in Phase 1, microcontroller from Phase 2      |
| Week 1   | Resistor, LED, and ceramic capacitor assortments | Cheap, and you will use them all year                |
| Week 16  | Raspberry Pi Debug Probe                     | Breakpoints and stepping on the Pico, via SWD and GDB      |
| Week 21  | USB logic analyzer (8-channel, sigrok-compatible) | Watch I²C, SPI, and UART on the wire                  |
| Week 27  | Soldering iron (a Pinecil is a good choice), solder, flux, brass tip cleaner | Soldering and PCB assembly |
| Later    | Oscilloscope                                 | Optional. Seeing analog signals over time; buy when you miss it |

* Safety and good habits

- Everything here runs from USB at 5 V or less. Keep it that way until Phase 6
  covers power properly. Mains voltage is out of scope for the whole plan.
- Disconnect power before changing wiring.
- The Pico's GPIO pins are *3.3 V*. Putting 5 V on a GPIO pin can damage the
  chip. Check every part's voltage before connecting it.
- A single GPIO pin should source or sink only a few milliamps. Anything
  bigger (motors, relays, long LED strips) goes through a transistor, which
  you learn in week 7.
- Solder in a ventilated space, wash your hands afterwards, and keep the iron
  in its stand.

* Books and courses used throughout

- Charles Platt, /Make: Electronics/ (3rd edition). Hands-on, experiment-led.
- Paul Scherz and Simon Monk, /Practical Electronics for Inventors/. The reference to look things up in.
- [[https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-002-circuits-and-electronics-spring-2007/][MIT OCW 6.002 Circuits and Electronics]]. Undergraduate theory, used as a reference.
- [[https://learn.sparkfun.com/tutorials][SparkFun tutorials]]. Short, practical explainers.
