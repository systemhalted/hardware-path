:PROPERTIES:
:WEEK: 5
:CUSTOM_ID: week-05

** Learn
A microcontroller is a whole small computer on one chip: CPU, RAM, flash, and
peripherals. It has no operating system unless you put one there. MicroPython
is firmware: a program you flash onto the Pico that runs a Python interpreter
on the chip itself. Your laptop only sends it code over USB. GPIO
(general-purpose input/output) pins are the chip's connection to the world:
each one can be set high (3.3 V) or low (0 V), or read as an input.

- Raspberry Pi: [[https://www.raspberrypi.com/documentation/microcontrollers/micropython.html][MicroPython on the Pico]] (flashing the UF2 file)
- [[https://datasheets.raspberrypi.com/pico/raspberry-pi-pico-python-sdk.pdf][Raspberry Pi Pico Python SDK]] (PDF), chapters 1–3
- [[https://docs.micropython.org/en/latest/rp2/quickref.html][MicroPython quick reference for the RP2]]
- [[https://thonny.org/][Thonny]] editor, or =mpremote= from the command line

* TODO Week 6: Digital output and pin limits
:PROPERTIES:
:WEEK: 6
:CUSTOM_ID: week-06
:END:
** Learn
A GPIO output is a tiny switch that connects the pin to 3.3 V or to ground.
It can only supply a little current: the RP2350 lets you configure the drive
strength (2, 4, 8, or 12 mA), with a total limit across all pins. Driving an
LED through a resistor sits comfortably inside those limits. Driving a motor
does not. Reading the electrical-characteristics section of a datasheet is
how you find these numbers, and it is a skill you will use for the rest of
the plan.

- [[https://datasheets.raspberrypi.com/picow/pico-2-w-datasheet.pdf][Pico 2 W datasheet]]: the pinout and power sections
- [[https://datasheets.raspberrypi.com/rp2350/rp2350-datasheet.pdf][RP2350 datasheet]]: search for "drive strength" in the GPIO chapter and the electrical specifications table
- SparkFun: [[https://learn.sparkfun.com/tutorials/logic-levels][Logic levels]]
** Build [0/5]
- [ ] Find the GPIO output drive-strength options and the default in the RP2350 datasheet; write them in your notes
- [ ] Connect an external LED with a resistor to a GPIO pin and blink it from code
- [ ] Measure the pin's voltage when high and when low, with the LED connected and without
- [ ] Build a traffic light (red, amber, green) with realistic timing
- [ ] Calculate the total current with all three LEDs on and compare with the chip's limits
** Parts
- Red, yellow, and green LEDs
- 220 Ω or 330 Ω resistors
** Checkpoint
Why does the pin's "high" voltage drop slightly when the LED is connected, and
why would it drop much further if you tried to drive a small motor directly?
#+begin_details
#+html: <summary>Check your answer</summary>
The output transistor inside the chip has some resistance, so the voltage
across it rises as more current flows. That leaves less at the pin. A motor
wants hundreds of milliamps, far more than the pin can supply. The voltage
would collapse, the motor wouldn't run, and you'd risk damaging the pin.
That's the problem week 7 solves.
#+end_details

* TODO Week 7: Transistors and MOSFETs as switches
:PROPERTIES:
:WEEK: 7
:CUSTOM_ID: week-07
:END:
** Learn
A transistor lets a small signal control a large current. A bipolar (NPN)
transistor is controlled by base current: you size a base resistor so the
transistor is fully on ("saturated"). A logic-level N-channel MOSFET is
controlled by gate voltage and draws almost no steady current from the pin.
Use it as a "low-side switch" between the load and ground. Motors and relay
coils are inductors: when you switch them off, they kick back a voltage spike
that can destroy the transistor. A flyback diode across the load gives that
energy somewhere safe to go. This week explains why every real project has a
transistor somewhere.

- SparkFun: [[https://learn.sparkfun.com/tutorials/transistors][Transistors]] (focus on "Applications I: switches")
- Platt, /Make: Electronics/, the transistor experiments
- Search a MOSFET datasheet for "V_GS(th)" and for the gate voltages R_DS(on) is specified at. The threshold is where the MOSFET barely starts to conduct; only an R_DS(on) figure at 2.5 V or 3.3 V guarantees it is fully on from a Pico pin. The popular IRLZ44N, for example, is only specified from 4 V up
** Build [0/5]
- [ ] Switch an LED through an NPN transistor (2N2222 or BC547) from a GPIO pin; calculate the base resistor first
- [ ] Measure the voltage across the transistor (collector to emitter) when on, and explain what "saturated" means from that number
- [ ] Drive a small DC motor from a separate supply (a 4×AA pack, about 6 V) through the MOSFET, with a flyback diode across the motor, a 100 µF capacitor across the motor supply, and the supply's ground joined to the Pico's ground. Don't run motors from the Pico's =VBUS=: startup and stall current can brown out the Pico or trip the laptop's USB port
- [ ] In the MOSFET's datasheet, find the gate threshold and the gate voltage its on-resistance is guaranteed at, and confirm it is fully on at 3.3 V
- [ ] Explain in your notes what the flyback diode protects and what happens without it (don't test this one)
** Parts
- NPN transistors: 2N2222 or BC547
- N-channel MOSFET with R_DS(on) specified at V_GS = 2.5 V, such as AO3400A or Si2302. These are tiny SOT-23 parts: buy them already mounted on a breakout board, since soldering comes in week 27
- Diodes: 1N4001 (flyback) and 1N4148
- Small 3–6 V DC motor
- 4×AA battery holder (separate motor supply) and a 100 µF electrolytic capacitor
- Resistors: 1 kΩ, 10 kΩ (gate pull-down)
** Checkpoint
Why do you put a 10 kΩ resistor from the MOSFET's gate to ground, and why does
the flyback diode go across the motor "backwards"?
#+begin_details
#+html: <summary>Check your answer</summary>
The pull-down holds the gate at 0 V while the Pico is booting or the pin is
an input. Otherwise the gate floats and the motor can twitch on at random.
The diode is reverse-biased in normal operation, so it carries no current.
When the MOSFET switches off, the motor's inductance drives current onward
and reverses the voltage across the motor. The diode then conducts and lets
that current circulate harmlessly instead of spiking the MOSFET.
#+end_details

* TODO Week 8: Digital input, pull-ups, and button bounce
:PROPERTIES:
:WEEK: 8
:CUSTOM_ID: week-08
:END:
** Learn
An input pin reads whether a voltage is closer to 3.3 V or to 0 V. A pin
connected to nothing "floats" and reads random values, because it picks up
noise. A pull-up or pull-down resistor gives it a defined default. The Pico
has built-in ones you enable in code. Mechanical buttons don't switch
cleanly: the contacts bounce for a few milliseconds, so one press can look
like several. Debouncing in hardware (an RC filter) or in software (ignore
changes for a short time) fixes it.

- SparkFun: [[https://learn.sparkfun.com/tutorials/pull-up-resistors][Pull-up resistors]]
- Jack Ganssle: [[https://www.ganssle.com/debouncing.htm][A guide to debouncing]] (part 1 is enough for now)
- [[https://docs.micropython.org/en/latest/rp2/quickref.html][MicroPython RP2 quick reference]]: the =Pin= section on =PULL_UP=
** Build [0/5]
- [ ] Read an input pin with nothing connected, printing its value in a loop, and watch it float when you touch the wire
- [ ] Wire a button to ground and use the internal pull-up; confirm pressed reads 0 and released reads 1
- [ ] Count presses with a loop that detects transitions; press 20 times and compare the count
- [ ] Add software debouncing and repeat the 20-press test
- [ ] Make the button toggle an LED on each press, not just while held
** Parts
- Tactile buttons
- LEDs and resistors
** Checkpoint
Why should an input pin never be left floating, and what is one software way
to handle button bounce?
#+begin_details
#+html: <summary>Check your answer</summary>
A floating input has no defined voltage. It picks up nearby electrical noise
and reads random highs and lows, which your program sees as phantom presses.
One software fix: after detecting a change, ignore further changes for about
20–50 ms, or only accept a new state after it has been stable for that long.
#+end_details

* TODO Week 9: State and timing: a reaction-time game
:PROPERTIES:
:WEEK: 9
:CUSTOM_ID: week-09
:END:
** Learn
Embedded programs usually loop forever, so you can't block on =sleep()= while
also watching a button. The fix is to track state and time explicitly: keep
a variable for what phase you're in, and use a monotonic millisecond clock
(=time.ticks_ms()= with =time.ticks_diff()=) to decide when to move on. This is
the same idea as an event loop in software, made visible by the hardware.

- [[https://docs.micropython.org/en/latest/library/time.html][MicroPython =time= module]]: =ticks_ms=, =ticks_diff=, and why they wrap
- Use your week 8 notes on debouncing
** Build [0/5]
- [ ] Sketch the game's states (waiting, get ready, go, result) and transitions on paper first
- [ ] After a random delay, light the LED; measure the time until the button press, and print it
- [ ] Handle a press before the LED lights as a false start
- [ ] Run the game without any blocking =sleep()= longer than a few milliseconds
- [ ] Add a "best time" that survives between rounds (but not power cycles)
** Parts
- One LED, one button, resistors
** Checkpoint
Why use =time.ticks_diff(now, start)= instead of =now - start=?
#+begin_details
#+html: <summary>Check your answer</summary>
The tick counter is a fixed-width number that wraps back to zero after its
maximum. Plain subtraction gives a wrong (even negative) result across a wrap.
=ticks_diff= does the arithmetic modulo the counter's range, so short
intervals are correct even when the counter wraps between the two readings.
#+end_details
