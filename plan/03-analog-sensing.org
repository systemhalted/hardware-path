* TODO Week 10: Analog-to-digital conversion
:PROPERTIES:
:WEEK: 10
:CUSTOM_ID: week-10
:END:
** Learn
The world is analog; the Pico counts. Its ADC samples a voltage between 0 V
and its reference (about 3.3 V) and turns it into a 12-bit number, 0–4095.
MicroPython's =read_u16()= scales that to 0–65535. Resolution is the voltage
per step (3.3 V / 4096 ≈ 0.8 mV), but accuracy is a separate question:
reference error, noise, and the source's resistance all show up in the
readings. A potentiometer is an adjustable voltage divider, so it's the
perfect first analog input.

- SparkFun: [[https://learn.sparkfun.com/tutorials/analog-to-digital-conversion][Analog-to-digital conversion]]
- [[https://docs.micropython.org/en/latest/library/machine.ADC.html][MicroPython =machine.ADC=]]
- [[https://datasheets.raspberrypi.com/picow/pico-2-w-datasheet.pdf][Pico 2 W datasheet]]: which pins are ADC inputs (GP26–GP28) and how ADC_VREF is generated
** Build [0/5]
- [ ] Wire a 10 kΩ potentiometer as a divider between 3V3 and GND, with the wiper on GP26
- [ ] Print the raw reading and the calculated voltage as you turn the knob
- [ ] Measure the wiper voltage with the meter at five positions and compare with the Pico's numbers
- [ ] Hold the knob still and record 100 readings; note the spread (that's noise)
- [ ] Average 16 readings per sample and compare the spread
** Parts
- 10 kΩ linear potentiometer (breadboard-friendly)
** Checkpoint
The ADC returns 2048 out of 4095 (12-bit). Roughly what voltage is on the pin,
and why might the meter disagree by a few tens of millivolts?
#+begin_details
#+html: <summary>Check your answer</summary>
About 3.3 × 2048 / 4095 ≈ 1.65 V. Differences come from the ADC's reference
not being exactly 3.3 V, the ADC's own offset and gain errors, noise on the
signal and supply, and the meter's accuracy. Absolute accuracy needs
calibration against a known voltage.
#+end_details

* TODO Week 11: PWM, duty cycle, and servos
:PROPERTIES:
:WEEK: 11
:CUSTOM_ID: week-11
:END:
** Learn
A GPIO pin can only be fully on or fully off. Pulse-width modulation switches
it on and off fast, and the fraction of time it's on (the duty cycle) sets the
average power. Your eye averages a fast-blinking LED into "dimmer", and a
motor's inertia averages it into "slower". Hobby servos use PWM differently:
the pulse *width* (about 1–2 ms, repeated every 20 ms) encodes a position.

- SparkFun: [[https://learn.sparkfun.com/tutorials/pulse-width-modulation][Pulse width modulation]]
- [[https://docs.micropython.org/en/latest/library/machine.PWM.html][MicroPython =machine.PWM=]]
- SparkFun: [[https://learn.sparkfun.com/tutorials/hobby-servo-tutorial][Hobby servo tutorial]]
** Build [0/5]
- [ ] Fade an LED with PWM, using the potentiometer from week 10 to set the duty cycle
- [ ] Measure the pin with the meter on DC volts at 25%, 50%, and 75% duty; explain the readings
- [ ] Lower the PWM frequency until you can see flicker; note the frequency
- [ ] Drive a micro servo (SG90-class) at 50 Hz and map the potentiometer to its angle
- [ ] Power the servo from a separate 4–6 V supply (the 4×AA pack from week 7), with a 100 µF capacitor across it and its ground joined to the Pico's; explain in your notes why neither 3V3 nor the Pico's =VBUS= is a good choice
** Parts

** Checkpoint
Your meter reads about 1.6 V on a PWM pin at 50% duty. Is the pin ever
actually at 1.6 V? Why does the meter show that?
#+begin_details
#+html: <summary>Check your answer</summary>
No. The pin is at 3.3 V half the time and 0 V the other half. The meter
averages over a time much longer than one PWM period, so it shows roughly
the average: 50% × 3.3 V ≈ 1.65 V.
#+end_details

* TODO Week 12: Light sensors and dividers
:PROPERTIES:
:WEEK: 12
:CUSTOM_ID: week-12
:END:
** Learn
A light-dependent resistor (photoresistor) changes resistance with light:
hundreds of ohms in bright light, hundreds of kilohms in the dark. The ADC
measures voltage, not resistance, so you put the LDR in a voltage divider
with a fixed resistor. Choosing that resistor sets which light levels you can
tell apart, which is your week 3 knowledge doing real work.

- SparkFun: [[https://learn.sparkfun.com/tutorials/photocell-hookup-guide][Photocell hookup guide]]
- SparkFun: [[https://learn.sparkfun.com/tutorials/voltage-dividers][Voltage dividers]] (review)
** Build [0/5]
- [ ] Measure the LDR's resistance in room light, under your hand, and under a phone flashlight
- [ ] Choose a fixed resistor so room light lands near the middle of the ADC range; show the calculation
- [ ] Build the divider on an ADC pin and print a light level from 0 to 100
- [ ] Swap the fixed resistor for one ten times larger and describe how the useful range moves
- [ ] Log readings for a few minutes as the room light changes (a lamp on and off is enough)
** Parts
- Photoresistors (LDRs, e.g. GL5528)
- Resistors: 1 kΩ, 10 kΩ, 100 kΩ
** Checkpoint
The LDR is 10 kΩ in room light. Why is a 10 kΩ fixed resistor a good choice
for the divider here?
#+begin_details
#+html: <summary>Check your answer</summary>
With equal resistances, room light puts the output at mid-range (about
1.65 V). That leaves room to move both up and down, and the divider's output
changes fastest near the middle. So small light changes around room level
give the biggest change in ADC counts.
#+end_details

* TODO Week 13: Noise, thresholds, and hysteresis
:PROPERTIES:
:WEEK: 13
:CUSTOM_ID: week-13
:END:
** Learn
Turn on a light "when it gets dark" with a single threshold, and near dusk
it will flicker on and off as the reading jitters across the line. The fix is
hysteresis: switch on below one level and off above a higher one, so noise
smaller than the gap can't toggle it. It's the same idea as a Schmitt
trigger in hardware and a debounce in software. Inputs are noisy, so a
technically correct threshold can still behave badly.

- Wikipedia: [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schmitt_trigger][Schmitt trigger]] (the first two sections, for the idea)
- Your week 12 log, to see the size of the noise
- Watch: MIT 6.002 [[https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-002-circuits-and-electronics-spring-2007/resources/lecture-21/][lecture 21, Op amps positive feedback]] (how positive feedback makes a Schmitt trigger)
** Build [0/5]
- [ ] Make a night light with one threshold and watch it chatter near the boundary (cover the LDR slowly)
- [ ] Measure the noise band from your logs and choose on and off levels wider than it
- [ ] Add hysteresis with separate on and off thresholds; repeat the slow-cover test
- [ ] Add a short delay that requires the condition to hold before switching, and compare
- [ ] Fade the LED with PWM instead of snapping on, using week 11
** Parts
- LDR circuit from week 12, LED, resistor
** Checkpoint
Trace the full path from a change in light to the LED turning on: name each
physical and software step.
#+begin_details
#+html: <summary>Check your answer</summary>
1. Less light raises the LDR's resistance.
2. The divider's output voltage changes (which way depends on whether the LDR
   is on the top or the bottom).
3. The ADC samples that voltage and converts it to a number.
4. Your code averages the readings and compares them with the "on" threshold.
5. Below it, the code sets the LED pin high or raises the PWM duty.
6. The pin drives current through the resistor and LED.
7. The LED stays on until the reading crosses the separate "off" threshold.
#+end_details
