* TODO Week 1: Voltage, current, resistance, and the breadboard
:PROPERTIES:
:WEEK: 1
:CUSTOM_ID: week-01
:END:
** Learn
Voltage is the push, current is the flow, resistance is what limits the flow.
Ohm's law (V = I × R) ties them together, and power (P = V × I) tells you how
much heat a part has to get rid of. Almost every calculation this year starts
from these two equations. The breadboard is the other foundation: knowing
which holes are connected saves hours of debugging.

- SparkFun: [[https://learn.sparkfun.com/tutorials/voltage-current-resistance-and-ohms-law][Voltage, current, resistance, and Ohm's law]]
- SparkFun: [[https://learn.sparkfun.com/tutorials/how-to-use-a-breadboard][How to use a breadboard]]
- SparkFun: [[https://learn.sparkfun.com/tutorials/how-to-use-a-multimeter][How to use a multimeter]]
- Platt, /Make: Electronics/, chapter 1
- Watch: MIT 6.002 [[https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-002-circuits-and-electronics-spring-2007/resources/lecture-1/][lecture 1, Introduction and lumped abstraction]]
- Watch: MIT 6.002 [[https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-002-circuits-and-electronics-spring-2007/resources/lecture-2/][lecture 2, Basic circuit analysis method]]

- [ ] Find the Pico's =3V3(OUT)= and =GND= pins on the pinout and measure the voltage between them with the Pico on USB

** Checkpoint
Your meter reads 3.28 V across a resistor and 3.3 mA through it. What is the
resistance, and how much power is the resistor turning into heat?
#+begin_details
#+html: <summary>Check your answer</summary>
R = V / I = 3.28 / 0.0033 ≈ 994 Ω, so a 1 kΩ resistor within tolerance.
P = V × I = 3.28 × 0.0033 ≈ 10.8 mW, far below a typical 250 mW (¼ W) rating.

* TODO Week 2: LEDs, polarity, and current limiting
:PROPERTIES:
:WEEK: 2
:CUSTOM_ID: week-02
:END:
** Learn
An LED is a diode: current flows one way only, and once it conducts it drops
a roughly fixed forward voltage (about 1.8–2.2 V for red, about 3 V for blue or
white). It has almost no resistance of its own, so without a series resistor
the current is limited only by the source, and the LED burns out. Sizing that
resistor is the first real design calculation you will make:
R = (V_supply − V_forward) / I_desired.

- SparkFun: [[https://learn.sparkfun.com/tutorials/light-emitting-diodes-leds][Light-emitting diodes]]
- SparkFun: [[https://learn.sparkfun.com/tutorials/diodes][Diodes]]
- Platt, /Make: Electronics/, the LED experiments in chapter 1
- Watch: MIT 6.002 [[https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-002-circuits-and-electronics-spring-2007/resources/lecture-6/][lecture 6, Nonlinear analysis]] (diodes as nonlinear parts; the math is optional)
** Build [0/5]
- [ ] Calculate a resistor for a red LED at about 10 mA from 3.3 V, and pick the nearest standard value at or above it
- [ ] Build the circuit and confirm the LED lights; then reverse the LED and note what happens
- [ ] Measure the voltage across the LED and across the resistor; check they add up to the supply
- [ ] Measure the actual current and compare it with your prediction
- [ ] Swap in a resistor twice as large; predict the new current and brightness, then measure
** Parts
- Red, green, and blue LEDs (5 mm)
- Resistors: 100 Ω, 150 Ω, 220 Ω, 330 Ω, 1 kΩ
** Checkpoint
A blue LED has a forward voltage of 3.0 V. Can you light it to a useful
brightness from 3.3 V with a 220 Ω resistor? What current would you expect?
#+begin_details
#+html: <summary>Check your answer</summary>
Only dimly. Just 0.3 V is left for the resistor, so I ≈ 0.3 / 220 ≈ 1.4 mA.
It will glow, but modern LEDs are visible at 1–2 mA. For more current you'd
use a smaller resistor, or a higher supply such as the 5 V =VBUS= pin with a
resistor recalculated for 5 V.
#+end_details

* TODO Week 3: Series, parallel, and voltage dividers
:PROPERTIES:
:WEEK: 3
:CUSTOM_ID: week-03
:END:
** Learn
In series, resistances add and the same current flows through each. In
parallel, the voltage is the same across each and the currents add. A voltage
divider is two resistors in series, with the output taken from the middle:
V_out = V_in × R2 / (R1 + R2). Dividers turn up everywhere: reading sensors,
scaling a voltage down for a 3.3 V input, and setting reference levels. They
also teach the idea of loading: what you connect to the output changes it.

- SparkFun: [[https://learn.sparkfun.com/tutorials/series-and-parallel-circuits][Series and parallel circuits]]
- SparkFun: [[https://learn.sparkfun.com/tutorials/voltage-dividers][Voltage dividers]]
- Watch: MIT 6.002 [[https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-002-circuits-and-electronics-spring-2007/resources/lecture-3/][lecture 3, Superposition, Thévenin and Norton]]
** Build [0/5]
- [ ] Put three resistors in series, predict the total resistance, then measure it
- [ ] Put the same three in parallel, predict, then measure
- [ ] Build a divider from two 10 kΩ resistors on 3.3 V; predict the output, then measure
- [ ] Change R2 to 4.7 kΩ; predict and measure again, and explain the change
- [ ] Put a 10 kΩ resistor across R2 (a "load") and explain why the output drops
** Parts
- Resistors: 1 kΩ, 4.7 kΩ, 10 kΩ (several of each)
** Checkpoint
A divider with R1 = 10 kΩ and R2 = 10 kΩ on 3.3 V gives 1.65 V. You connect a
10 kΩ load across R2. What is the new output, and what does this tell you
about choosing divider values?
#+begin_details
#+html: <summary>Check your answer</summary>
R2 in parallel with the load is 5 kΩ, so V_out = 3.3 × 5 / (10 + 5) = 1.1 V.
A divider only holds its voltage when the load's resistance is much larger
than the divider's (about ten times or more). Pick divider resistors much
smaller than the load, or buffer the output.
#+end_details

* TODO Week 4: Switches, ground, continuity, and schematics
:PROPERTIES:
:WEEK: 4
:CUSTOM_ID: week-04
:END:
** Learn
A schematic is the circuit's source code: it says what connects to what,
not where parts sit. Learn the symbols for resistors, LEDs, switches,
batteries, and ground. Ground is simply the reference point all voltages are
measured from; every part of a circuit that talks to another part needs a
shared ground. Switches come in types (SPST, SPDT, momentary, latching), and a
four-legged tactile button has pins that are connected in pairs, which trips
up nearly everyone once.

- SparkFun: [[https://learn.sparkfun.com/tutorials/how-to-read-a-schematic][How to read a schematic]]
- SparkFun: [[https://learn.sparkfun.com/tutorials/button-and-switch-basics][Button and switch basics]]
- Platt, /Make: Electronics/, the switch experiments in chapter 2
** Build [0/5]
- [ ] Use continuity mode to work out which legs of a tactile button are always connected and which close when pressed
- [ ] Draw a schematic of a button that turns on an LED, before building anything
- [ ] Build it from your schematic only
- [ ] Add a second button so that either button lights the LED; draw it first
- [ ] Change it so both buttons must be pressed; explain the difference in schematic terms (parallel vs series)
** Parts
- Tactile push buttons (6 mm, four legs)
- LEDs and resistors from earlier weeks
** Checkpoint
Draw (in your notes) an LED circuit without copying a diagram. Then answer:
your LED doesn't light. In what order would you check things, and which meter
mode do you use for each check?
#+begin_details
#+html: <summary>Check your answer</summary>
A sensible order, with power on unless noted:
1. Supply voltage at the breadboard rails (DC volts).
2. Voltage across the LED and across the resistor (DC volts). All the supply
   across the LED means it's reversed or open. All of it across the resistor
   means the LED is shorted.
3. With power off, check each connection with continuity mode, especially
   that the parts sit in different breadboard rows.
4. The LED's polarity: the long leg and the round side go to positive.
#+end_details
